Francesco Landini

c. 1325–1397 · The Italian Trecento — Florence's Blind Master

The organist crowned with laurel, celebrated poet and instrument-maker whose sweet two- and three-voice ballate gave the fourteenth century one of its most tender sounds — and lent his name to a cadence that would charm all Europe.

History of Early Music · No. 05 — The Trecento

01 — THE MAN

Who Was Francesco Landini?

Francesco Landini (c. 1325–1397) was the greatest composer of the Italian Trecento — the fourteenth-century flowering of secular art-song that is Italy's answer to the French Ars Nova. Blinded by smallpox in childhood, he became the most admired musician in Florence: a virtuoso organist, poet, and maker of instruments, publicly crowned with the laurel wreath of a poet.

03 — THE ERA

The Italian Trecento

Trecento — literally "three hundred", the Italian shorthand for the 1300s — names both a century and a musical golden age. While France pursued the intricate rhythms of the Ars Nova, Italy grew its own refined, lyrical polyphony: graceful, singable, and unmistakably Italian in its sweetness.

The Italian way

Flowing melody in the top voice, warm and vocal; long ornamental runs; a taste for consonant thirds and sixths that would soon sound "sweet" all over Europe.

The French Ars Nova

More abstract and cerebral — isorhythm, complex proportional rhythms, the great motets of Machaut. Late in the century its notation would absorb Italy's.

The Trecento repertory is overwhelmingly secular — love songs and dances in the vernacular, not liturgical Latin. Landini stands at its very summit.

04 — THE ORGANETTO

The Blind Organist Crowned With Laurel

Landini's fame rested first on his playing. His instrument was the organetto (portative organ): a small keyboard organ held on the lap, the left hand working the bellows while the right hand played the melody. It is the instrument shown beside him in his manuscript portrait and carved on his tomb.

A famous scene in Giovanni da Prato's Il Paradiso degli Alberti (written c. 1425) recalls Landini playing his organetto in a Florentine garden on a summer's day — and the company falling silent, spellbound, as if the very birds gathered to listen. For his artistry he was crowned, like a poet, with laurel.

His manuscript portrait appears at the head of his section in the great Squarcialupi Codex, organetto in hand — the image by which we still picture him.

05 — THE FORMS

Song-Forms of the Trecento

Trecento composers worked in three great secular forms. Landini touched all of them, but poured his genius above all into the last.

Madrigal

The earlier Trecento form: two or three stanzas set to the same music, capped by a contrasting ritornello. Often pastoral verse.

Caccia

A "chase": two voices in strict canon over a slower tenor, the words painting hunts, fishing or bustling markets — music as lively picture.

Ballata

Descended from a sung dance. By Landini's day the leading form of Italian love-song — and the heart of his art.

Of Landini's roughly 150 surviving pieces, the overwhelming majority are ballate, alongside a smaller group of madrigals.

06 — THE BALLATA

The Ballata — Landini's Form

The ballata began as music for dancing (from ballare, "to dance") and grew into a refined song of courtly love, for two or three voices. Its shape is a neat rounded scheme built on a refrain, the ripresa, that returns to frame each verse.

The volta reuses the music of the ripresa, so the tune circles back on itself — a satisfying rounded shape that made the ballata perfect for song.

08 — THE SWEET CADENCE

The Landini Cadence — Reading the Turn

At a cadence, medieval melody usually pushed the leading tone (7) straight up to the octave (8). Landini's signature is a graceful detour: the top voice first dips down to the sixth (6), then leaps up a third to the octave — the sweet 7–6–8 "under-third" turn that bears his name.

How to read it: follow the top voice left to right. After the leading tone (7, amber) it does not resolve straight up; it drops to the 6th (6, blue), then springs up a third to the octave (8, green) as the lower voice settles onto the final. That little dip-and-leap is the Landini cadence.

10 — THE GREAT SOURCE

The Squarcialupi Codex

Almost everything we have of Landini reaches us through one magnificent book. The Squarcialupi Codex — a lavish, gold-illuminated Florentine anthology of Trecento song, copied in the early 1400s — is the single greatest source of Italian fourteenth-century music. It is named for the later Florentine organist Antonio Squarcialupi, who once owned it.

Landini's book

Its largest section by far is devoted to Landini — around 145 of his works, more than any other composer in the manuscript.

His portrait

An illuminated miniature heads his section, depicting Landini seated with his portative organ — a rare medieval "author portrait".

A gallery of masters

It preserves many Trecento composers, each with a portrait — a visual roll-call of Italy's musical golden age.

Without this book, Landini's name would survive but most of his music would be lost — a reminder how thin the thread of medieval survival can be.

11 — READING THE PAGE

Italian Trecento Notation

Italy wrote its rhythm differently from France. In the system set out by the theorist Marchetto da Padova (c. 1318), a breve was split into groups of smaller notes called divisiones — and a letter marked how many notes each group held. Dots kept the groups apart, in place of bar-lines.

How to read it: find the little letter marking the divisione — it says how many notes fill one breve. Read each dot-bounded group as a single beat's worth of notes. Late in the century Italian scribes increasingly borrowed the French mensural signs, and the two systems merged.

12 — THE MUSIC

Songs That Survive

Landini's ballate range from sunlit and dancing to grave and grieving — always with that singing top line and the sweet cadences that are his fingerprint. A few have become the best-known music of the whole Trecento.

Ecco la primavera

"Here comes the spring" — a joyful two-voice ballata, his most famous song and a byword for Trecento freshness.

Gran piant' agli occhi

"Great weeping to the eyes" — a tender, sorrowing love-ballata showing his more inward, expressive vein.

Non avrà ma' pietà

"She will never have pity" — a much-admired ballata of courtly longing, elegant and finely wrought.

♪ Gran piant' agli occhi ♪ Non avrà ma' pietà

These are worldly love-songs in Tuscan Italian for two or three voices — chamber music for the cultured circles of late-Trecento Florence.

13 — LISTENING GUIDE

How to Listen to Landini

  • Follow the top voice — it carries the tune, high and ornamental, over its supporting voices.
  • Wait for the cadences: catch the little dip-and-leap of the Landini turn as each phrase comes to rest.
  • Feel the triple lilt of the ballata's dance origins beneath the flowing melody.
  • Notice how consonant it sounds — the thirds and sixths that make Trecento music feel so sweet to modern ears.

14 — LEGACY

The Sweetness That Spread

Landini gathered the Italian Trecento into its finest achievement and left it a lasting gift. His cadence travelled north into the songs of the next generation; his ballate remain the best-loved music of the century; and his portrait, organetto in hand, has become the very face of the age. When Florence buried him at San Lorenzo in 1397, an era of song went with him.

The cadence

The 7–6–8 "Landini cadence" outlived him by generations, sweetening cadences across all of fifteenth-century Europe.

The ballata

He brought the two- and three-voice ballata to its peak; his songs are the summit of Trecento secular art.

The image

Blind organist crowned with laurel, he embodies the meeting of music, poetry and the new humanism of Florence.
