Claudio Monteverdi

1567–1643 · The Doorway from Renaissance to Baroque

The composer who declared that the words must rule the music — and in doing so loosed dissonance from its chains, forged the recitative, and gave Europe its first great opera.

History of Early Music · No. 17 — The Threshold of the Baroque

01 — THE MAN

A Composer on the Threshold

Claudio Monteverdi (1567–1643) stands astride the great divide of Western music. Trained in the flawless polyphony of the late Renaissance, he broke it open from within — making music that speaks, that argues and weeps and rages — and carried the art into the age we call the Baroque.

03 — THE TWO COURTS

Mantua & Venice

Monteverdi's career unfolds between two great Italian centres — the private splendour of a ducal court, and the public grandeur of the greatest church in the Republic of Venice.

The Gonzaga Court, Mantua

Here, in the service of the ruling Gonzaga family, Monteverdi matured as a madrigalist and wrote L'Orfeo for a courtly audience.

Court music was intimate and lavish at once — made to dazzle a prince and his guests in a single hall.

St Mark's, Venice

From 1613 he was maestro di cappella at St Mark's — the summit of church musicianship in Italy — for some thirty years.

Its galleries and organs invited music of splendour; Venice also gave him its new public opera houses.

The move from Mantua to Venice is also a move from the closed world of the court to the open, ticket-buying public of a great city.

04 — THE QUARREL

The Artusi Controversy

Around 1600 the conservative theorist Giovanni Maria Artusi attacked Monteverdi's madrigals for their unprepared, "incorrect" dissonances — sounds that broke the rules of Renaissance counterpoint. Monteverdi answered not with an apology but with a manifesto.

Artusi's charge

The dissonances are errors: they violate the settled laws of counterpoint that govern how voices may clash and resolve.

Monteverdi's reply

They are no errors but expression: the harshness serves the meaning of the words. A new practice needs new liberties.

The exchange, aired in print, is one of the most famous debates in music history — the old rules against a new expressive freedom.

05 — THE DOCTRINE

Prima Pratica & Seconda Pratica

Monteverdi did not reject the old art — he named it and set it beside a new one. Two practices, he argued, could stand side by side: the perfect polyphony of the past, and a freer music ruled by the word.

Prima pratica

The "first practice" — the flawless vocal counterpoint of the Renaissance, epitomised by Palestrina, where the music governs and the harmony obeys its own strict laws.

Seconda pratica

The "second practice" — where the words govern: the sense and feeling of the text may license dissonance and freedom that the old rules forbade.

"The text should be the mistress of the harmony, and not its servant."

— the guiding principle of the seconda pratica, set out by Giulio Cesare Monteverdi (the composer's brother) in defence of Claudio, 1607
06 — THE MADRIGALS

The Madrigal Transformed

Across his later books of madrigals (Books 4–8) Monteverdi carries the form from a cappella polyphony toward dramatic song: solo voices, expressive dissonance, and eventually instruments and a basso continuo.

Books 4 & 5

The daring dissonances that drew Artusi's fire; Book 5 begins to admit a written basso continuo.

Books 6 & 7

Laments, duets and concertato textures — voices paired with instruments, the madrigal becoming chamber drama.

Book 8

Madrigali guerrieri et amorosi — "madrigals of war and love" — the summa of his dramatic style, home of the stile concitato.

The madrigal is Monteverdi's laboratory: a private, word-driven form in which he tests every idea that will remake music.

07 — A NEW STYLE

Stile Concitato — the Agitated Style

In Book 8 Monteverdi announced his invention of the stile concitato ("agitated style"): to depict warlike passion and anger, a single note is repeated in a rapid, drumming stream of quick equal notes. For the first time, music sets out to portray the fury of the soul directly.

08 — THE FIRST OPERA

L'Orfeo & the Birth of Opera

Staged at Mantua in 1607, L'Orfeo tells the myth of Orpheus, who charms the underworld with his song. It is the earliest opera still regularly performed — a whole drama carried by music alone. It opens with a blazing brass Toccata: a bright fanfare, sounded three times, over a single held bass.

Synthesised brass · a ringing C-major fanfare over a sustained bass drone — the curtain-raiser of opera itself

A sung drama

Recitative, aria, chorus and dance are welded into a single continuous theatrical whole.

A vivid orchestra

A large, varied band of strings, brass and continuo instruments colours each scene and character.

Music as feeling

Orpheus's plea to the underworld shows music itself as the power that moves the heart.

09 — THE NEW SONG

Recitative & Monody

Opera needed a way to make a single voice tell a story. The answer was monody: one solo voice singing a flexible, speech-like line over a simple sustaining bass. In recitative the melody bends to the rhythm and rise-and-fall of the words — halfway between speech and song.

One voice, one line

After centuries of interwoven polyphony, a single melody carries the drama, freed to follow the text exactly.

A supporting bass

Beneath it, the basso continuo holds the harmony — present but subordinate, so the words stay clear.

Speech made music

Recitative mirrors the natural cadence of Italian speech; the aria blossoms where feeling overflows into melody.

Monody was pioneered by the Florentine Camerata circle; Monteverdi seized it and gave it dramatic depth and lyric beauty.

10 — NOTATION · I

Reading the Basso Continuo

The Baroque runs on the basso continuo: a single written bass line, played by a low instrument, with small figures (numbers) beneath telling a keyboard or lute player which chords to build above it. The player "realises" the shorthand into full harmony on the spot. Here is how to read it.

How to read it: read the bass note first, then its figure. A blank or "5" means the plain triad (a 5th and a 3rd above the bass); "6" means use the 6th instead of the 5th (a first-inversion chord); "7" adds a 7th; stacked figures like "6 4" name the exact intervals to stack. The player fills in the rest by taste — no two realisations are quite alike.

11 — NOTATION · II

The Arrival of Barlines

Renaissance polyphony was written without barlines — each voice flowed in its own part-book, its rhythm shaped by the words. As music became more chordal and dance-driven, the score gained regular barlines and a governing metre: a steady pulse grouped into measures of equal length.

How to read it: the time signature (here 3/4) sets how many beats fill each measure; the vertical barlines mark off those equal measures, and a thick double bar closes the piece. The first beat after each barline carries a natural stress — the "downbeat" that gives Baroque music its dancing, driving pulse.

12 — SACRED SUMMIT

The Vespers of 1610

The Vespro della Beata Vergine — the "Vespers of the Blessed Virgin" of 1610 — is Monteverdi's great sacred monument. It gathers the whole range of his art into one collection: ancient chant, dazzling virtuoso solos, echoing double choirs and instrumental sonatas, bound together over a basso continuo.

Old & new

Plainchant melodies (the cantus firmus) anchor music of the most modern, theatrical brilliance.

Voices & instruments

Soloists, choirs and a colourful instrumental band answer each other across the space of the church.

Sacred drama

The expressive devices of opera — echo, ornament, the affetti of the text — are turned to worship.

Published while he was still at Mantua, the Vespers helped win him the coveted post at St Mark's in Venice.

13 — THE LATE OPERAS

The Venetian Operas

In old age, with Venice's new public opera houses opening, Monteverdi returned to the stage. Il ritorno d'Ulisse in patria and L'incoronazione di Poppea (1643) — one of the first operas on a historical rather than mythological subject — crown his life's work. Poppea closes with the sublime love duet Pur ti miro ("I gaze on you"), two voices entwining over a gently descending bass.

That an opera should end not with the virtuous rewarded but with two lovers (Nero and Poppea) in ecstasy is itself startlingly new.

14 — LISTENING GUIDE

How to Listen to Monteverdi

  • Follow the words — Monteverdi's music is built to serve them; feel how a dissonance lands exactly on a word of pain.
  • Listen for the basso continuo murmuring beneath the voice — the harmonic ground on which everything is built.
  • Feel the stile concitato quicken the pulse in the warlike passages, then relax into tender song.
  • In the operas, notice the shift between recitative (speech-like) and aria (lyrical) as feeling rises and falls.

15 — LEGACY

The Door He Opened

Monteverdi took the perfected polyphony of the Renaissance and turned it toward the future. From his hands come the seconda pratica's licence of expression, the basso continuo that would underpin all Baroque music, the recitative and the opera. Every opera composer from Cavalli to Handel — and beyond — walks through the door he opened.

Opera

L'Orfeo and Poppea found a living art form; sung drama has never since fallen silent.

Expression

The idea that music exists to move the passions and serve the word shapes everything that follows.

Harmony

The basso continuo and figured bass become the engine of Baroque music for a century and a half.

"The text should be the mistress of the harmony, and not its servant."

— the motto of the seconda pratica, set out by Giulio Cesare Monteverdi in defence of his brother Claudio, 1607
