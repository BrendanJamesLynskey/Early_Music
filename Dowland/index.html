John Dowland

1563–1626 · The English Lute Song & the Art of Melancholy

The supreme composer of the English ayre — who turned sorrow into the most exquisite art of the Elizabethan and Jacobean age, and whose falling "tears" still drop, note by note, four centuries on.

History of Early Music · The English Ayre & the Lute

01 — THE MAN

Who Was John Dowland?

Born in 1563, Dowland was the age's greatest virtuoso of the lute and the supreme composer of the ayre — the accompanied English solo song. Widely travelled and long denied a post at the English court, he served abroad before finally becoming one of the King's Lutes under James I. Above all he cultivated an artful melancholy that became his signature and his brand.

03 — THE GENRE

What Is an "Ayre"?

The ayre (air) is the English solo song of Dowland's generation: a single, shapely melody carried by one voice, supported by the lute. Where the Italian madrigal spun its poetry across several interweaving voices, the ayre keeps the words clear, the top line singable, and the mood intimate.

One voice + lute

A soloist sings the tune; the lute weaves an accompaniment beneath — chamber music for a single listener.

Words first

Clear, syllabic text-setting lets the poetry — love, loss, longing — be heard and felt directly.

Strophic + through-composed

Many ayres repeat the music for each verse; the darkest, like his laments, follow the words freely.

The English ayre is the direct ancestor of the art song — Schubert's Lied and beyond all descend from this intimate marriage of poem and accompaniment.

04 — THE SONGBOOK

The First Booke of Songes (1597)

Dowland's First Booke of Songes or Ayres (1597) was a publishing sensation, reprinted again and again. Its genius lay partly in a clever page layout: each opening was printed so the piece could be performed either by a single singer with lute, or by four singers seated around a table, each reading their own part from where they sat. One book, two ways to make music.

This "table-book" format made the ayre sociable — a whole household or company of friends could gather round and sing.

Each singer reads a part turned toward their own chair: solo song and part-song from one printed opening.

05 — THE INSTRUMENT

The Lute

The lute was the Renaissance's most beloved domestic instrument — a rounded, pear-shaped body of thin wooden ribs, a flat soundboard pierced by a carved rose, and a pegbox bent sharply back. Its strings run in paired courses, plucked with the fingers; the neck carries gut frets tied around it.

Courses

Strings run mostly in pairs (a "course"), tuned in unison or octaves for a full, ringing sound.

Frets

Movable gut frets are tied around the neck — the player can even adjust the tuning of a fret.

The rose

An intricately carved soundhole rosette, unique to each instrument, cut straight into the belly.

Plucked

Fingers, not a plectrum: this lets the lutenist voice several parts at once, like a tiny keyboard.

Because the lute plays chords, melody and bass together, one player can accompany a singer — the whole ayre lives on a single instrument.

06 — NOTATION · TABLATURE

How to Read Lute Tablature

Lute music is written not in staff notes but in tablature — a picture of the fingerboard. The lines are not pitches: they are the lute's courses (strings). A letter on a line tells you which fret to stop; the flags above give the rhythm.

Lines = courses

Six lines = six courses of strings. The top line is the highest-sounding course — not a pitch on a staff.

Letters = frets

a = open string, b = 1st fret, c = 2nd fret, and so on up the alphabet. Stack letters = a chord, plucked together.

Flags = rhythm

The little stems and flags above the staff give the timing; a flag holds until the next one appears.

07 — THE MASTERPIECE

Flow, My Tears

Flow, my tears (also known by its Latin name Lachrimae) is Dowland's most famous ayre and one of the best-loved songs of the age. It opens with a slow, falling figure — the musical image of tears dropping — that became so celebrated it circulated across Europe and was borrowed, quoted and arranged by countless musicians.

The song's four opening words — "Flow, my tears, fall" — are set to that very descent, so the music enacts the meaning of the poem.

08 — NOTATION · THE MOTIF

The "Tear" Motif: a Falling Tetrachord

The emotional signature of Lachrimae is a falling tetrachord — four notes descending stepwise, A–G–F–E. This gentle four-step slide downward was, for the age, the very sound of grief. Read it left to right on the staff:

09 — THE OTHER SIDE

Come Again, Sweet Love

Dowland was not only sorrow. Come again, sweet love doth now invite is his most beguiling love-ayre — and it holds a famous stroke of word-painting. On the words "to see, to hear, to touch, to kiss, to die" the melody rises stepwise, five little climbing notes of mounting desire, the exact opposite of the falling tear.

Where Flow my tears falls, Come again rises: the same composer's two directions of feeling, drawn as musical shapes.

10 — THE INSTRUMENTAL BOOK

Lachrimae, or Seaven Teares (1604)

In 1604 Dowland gathered his tear-music into a great instrumental collection: Lachrimae, or Seaven Teares, figured in seaven passionate pavans. It takes the falling motif of Flow my tears and spins from it seven stately pavans for a consort of viols (or violins) with lute — a whole meditation built on a single descending figure.

♪ Listen: Lachrimae (recording)

The pavan is a slow, grave processional dance in duple time — the perfect vessel for Dowland's contemplative sorrow.

11 — THE DARKEST AYRE

In Darkness Let Me Dwell

If Flow my tears is beautiful sorrow, In darkness let me dwell is Dowland at his most extreme — a through-composed lament of astonishing intensity, its harmony restless and its voice, at the close, simply breaking off unresolved. It is one of the most searching songs in all early music: melancholy pushed to the edge of despair.

Through-composed

No repeating verses — the music follows every turn of the poem's descent into darkness.

Anguished harmony

Dissonances lean and clash, colouring words of grief far beyond the sweet consonance of the madrigal.

The unfinished close

The voice ends by repeating its opening words and stopping mid-thought — grief left hanging in the air.

12 — MELANCHOLY AS ART

Semper Dowland, Semper Dolens

In Elizabethan and Jacobean England, melancholy was not merely gloom — it was a fashionable pose of the thoughtful, the poetic, the deep-souled. Dowland made it his artistic signature and even his personal brand, coining the Latin pun on his own name: Semper Dowland, semper dolens — "always Dowland, always grieving".

Semper Dowland, semper dolens.

— John Dowland's motto, punning on his own name: "Always Dowland, always grieving"

To be melancholy, in this age, was to be a person of feeling and refinement — and no one gave that mood a more exquisite voice than Dowland.

13 — THE WANDERING LUTENIST

Abroad, and Home at Last

For much of his life Dowland's fame ran ahead of his fortunes. Passed over for a post at the English court, he made his career on the Continent — most notably as a lavishly paid lutenist to King Christian IV of Denmark — before finally, under James I, being appointed one of the King's Lutes, the royal recognition he had long sought.

The Danish years

At the court of Christian IV, Dowland was among the highest-paid musicians in Europe — proof of the lutenist's international renown.

One of the King's Lutes

In 1612 he gained a place in the royal music of James I of England — the home appointment that had eluded him for decades.

A musician's life in this age depended wholly on patronage; Dowland's travels map the courts wealthy enough to prize a virtuoso.

14 — LISTENING GUIDE

How to Listen to Dowland

  • Follow the words — the ayre keeps text clear, so let the poem and the melody move together.
  • Feel the falling shapes in the sad songs and the rising ones in the joyful — the music draws the feeling.
  • Listen to the lute beneath the voice: it is not mere backing but a second, conversing part.
  • Notice how a single motif — the "tear" — can generate a whole song and even a whole book of pavans.

15 — LEGACY

The Sweetest Sorrow

Dowland stands at the golden close of the English Renaissance — the last, greatest master of an intimate art that would soon give way to the Baroque. His ayres perfected the union of poem and accompaniment that the whole later history of song would inherit; his falling "tears" became a shared European language of feeling; and his lute music remains the summit of the instrument's repertoire. In him, melancholy became one of music's most beautiful gifts.

The art song

The ayre's marriage of voice and instrument is the direct ancestor of the Lied and every song recital since.

The lute repertoire

His pavans, galliards and fantasias remain the heart of what lutenists play — and the model of the instrument's poetry.

Melancholy's voice

Dowland proved that sorrow, shaped by art, could be as moving and as sought-after as joy.

Semper Dowland, semper dolens — "Always Dowland, always grieving."
