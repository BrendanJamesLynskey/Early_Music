Josquin des Prez

c. 1450/55–1521 · The High Renaissance

The composer who turned imitation into the very grammar of music — where each phrase of text is passed voice to voice like a caught light — and whom Luther called "the master of the notes".

History of Early Music · No. 11 — The High Renaissance

01 — THE MAN

Who Was Josquin?

Josquin des Prez (Josquin Lebloitte dit Desprez) was the most famous and most influential composer of his age — a Franco-Flemish master who carried the northern art of counterpoint into Italy and France, and whose reputation, magnified by the new printing press, made him the first true celebrity of Western music.

02 — A LIFE IN MUSIC

A Career Across Europe

Josquin's movements are only partly documented, and scholarship has revised them repeatedly — but the broad arc is clear: a northern singer drawn south to the wealthy courts and chapels of Italy, then home again.

03 — THE BIG IDEA

Pervasive Imitation

Josquin's signature is the point of imitation: for each new phrase of text he invents a short motif, then hands it from voice to voice — each entering in turn, staggered in time and at a different pitch. The listener hears the same idea chase itself through the texture before the voices merge into a cadence, and a new point begins. It is music built phrase by phrase, from the words outward.

A motif per phrase

Each line of text gets its own little melodic idea, shaped to the rhythm and meaning of the words.

Voices enter in turn

One voice states the motif; the others answer, one after another, usually a fourth or fifth apart.

Equal partners

No single voice dominates. All four (or more) share the same material — a texture of true equality.

This "imitative" texture, perfected by Josquin's generation, became the default of Renaissance polyphony for a century — the sound of Palestrina, Lassus and Byrd.

05 — THE PARADIGM

Ave Maria … virgo serena

If one work defines the new imitative art, it is Josquin's motet Ave Maria … virgo serena. It became a textbook example of the style — its opening a limpid cascade of paired voices, each phrase introduced by one voice and answered by the next.

As the text unfolds, Josquin varies the texture with the meaning: flowing imitation for narrative, sudden homophony — all voices together — for moments of address, and a hushed, direct plea at the close. Craft and devotion move as one.

Why it mattered

A model of how to build a whole motet from successive points of imitation, each on a fresh motif shaped to its words.

It circulated widely in manuscript and print, teaching a generation of composers the imitative idiom.

06 — MUSIC & MEANING

Painting the Words

Josquin was a supreme setter of text. Beyond imitation, he bent the music to the sense of the words — an early, expressive flowering of what later theorists would call word-painting. Rhythm follows Latin accent; rising lines lift at words of ascent; the texture thins to a whisper at moments of grief or intimacy.

Rhythm from speech

The music's stresses match the natural accentuation of the Latin, so the words are clearly declaimed.

Texture as meaning

Full imitation for flowing narrative; block chords (homophony) to make a key phrase leap out.

Expressive extremes

In laments like Absalon fili mi, dark low writing and pained harmony carry the grief of the text.

Contemporaries prized exactly this marriage of sound and sense — Josquin's ability, as later writers put it, to make the music serve the emotion of the words.

07 — THE CHANSON

Mille regretz — a Falling Sigh

Josquin's melancholy chanson Mille regretz ("a thousand regrets") became so beloved that tradition dubbed it "the song of the emperor" — a favourite, it is said, of Charles V. Its pathos lives in a simple gesture: a phrase that sighs downward, the melody sinking by step as if under the weight of its own sorrow.

08 — THE GREAT MASSES

The Mass Cycles

The Mass was the grandest canvas of the age, and Josquin's cycles range across every technique of the day — from strict borrowing of a tune to free paraphrase of a chant, always bent to expressive ends.

10 — PRINT & FAME

The First Musical Celebrity

Josquin's career coincided with a revolution: music printing. In Venice, Ottaviano Petrucci issued the first collections of polyphony from movable type, and in 1502 devoted an entire book to Josquin's Masses alone — later followed by second and third books. No composer had been so honoured.

Print carried his music across Europe and made his name a selling point. Publishers even attached "Josquin" to works he never wrote — the surest sign that he had become a brand.

A famous jibe

A quip of the age ran that "now that Josquin is dead, he is putting out more works than when he was alive" — publishers cashing in on his name.

The remark is widely reported; treat it as an anecdote of his posthumous fame rather than a documented saying.

Petrucci's Misse Josquin (Venice, 1502) is often cited as the first printed book devoted to a single named composer — a landmark in music publishing.

11 — MASTER MOURNS MASTER

The Lament for Ockeghem

When the elder master Johannes Ockeghem died in 1497, Josquin wrote Nymphes des bois (the Déploration de Johannes Ockeghem) — a lament of extraordinary poise. Over the French poem, the tenor intones the plainchant Requiem, binding the new secular grief to the ancient chant for the dead. It is one composer's homage to another, and a portrait of a whole tradition mourning itself.

A déploration

A memorial song lamenting a departed master — a genre passed down the Franco-Flemish generations.

Chant within song

A borrowed Requiem chant sounds in one voice while the others sing the French poem of loss.

12 — LISTENING GUIDE

How to Listen to Josquin

  • Catch each point of imitation — a new motif, then hear the voices enter one by one to answer it.
  • Notice the shift to homophony — when the voices suddenly line up in chords, a key phrase is being underlined.
  • Follow the words — the rhythm hugs the Latin accent; the mood of the text shapes the sound.
  • Feel the equality of voices — no single tune on top; the interest is spread through the whole weave.

13 — THE JOSQUIN PROBLEM

Fame & the Problem of Attribution

Josquin's very celebrity created a puzzle for scholars. Because his name sold books, publishers and scribes attached it to music by lesser hands, and the total of works credited to him swelled far beyond what he can have written. Modern research has quietly shrunk the canon, stripping away doubtful pieces to recover the authentic Josquin — a reminder of how fame can blur the record.

Too many works

Far more music was attributed to him than he could have composed — the price of a marketable name.

Careful sifting

Scholars weigh sources, style and datings to separate the genuine from the merely "Josquin".

A leaner Josquin

The secure canon is now smaller — and, many argue, all the more astonishing for it.

The "Josquin problem" is a classic case study in musicology: how to establish authorship when fame itself has corrupted the sources.

14 — LEGACY

The Master of the Notes

Josquin fused northern counterpoint with Italian clarity and an unprecedented attention to the meaning of words, and left the imitative style as the common language of the century that followed. Through print his name outlived him as no composer's had before. In him the Renaissance found its Michelangelo of music — a reputation Martin Luther, a devoted admirer, put memorably.

Imitation

Pervasive imitation became the default texture of Renaissance polyphony for a hundred years.

Word & tone

His care for text and expression pointed the way to the madrigal and beyond.

Print & fame

The first composer whose name alone sold books — the birth of musical celebrity.
