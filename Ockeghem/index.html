Johannes Ockeghem

c. 1410 – 1497 · Franco-Flemish · The Generation Between Du Fay & Josquin

The great architect of seamless, long-breathed polyphony and the supreme puzzle-maker of the age — whose voices flow without pause and whose canons hide four singers inside two written lines.

History of Early Music · No. 10 — The Franco-Flemish Masters

01 — INTRODUCTION

Who Was Ockeghem?

Johannes Ockeghem (c. 1410–1497) was the towering Franco-Flemish master of the middle Renaissance — the leading composer of the generation that links Guillaume Du Fay to Josquin des Prez. A celebrated bass singer, he served three kings of France and was revered less for grand landmarks than for the quality of his sound: music that flows on and on, and craft of dazzling ingenuity.

02 — A LIFE AT COURT

Servant of Three Kings

Born in the Franco-Flemish lands (the region of Hainaut/Flanders), Ockeghem spent the heart of his career in the service of the French crown, rising to the honoured post of maistre de chapelle and treasurer of the Abbey of Saint-Martin at Tours. He outlived most of his contemporaries and died full of years and fame in 1497.

03 — THE SOUND

The Endless Melody

Ockeghem's signature is a seamless flow. Where earlier music arrived in neat phrases with firm cadences, his voices are spun into long, irregular, overlapping arcs. Just as one line seems about to close, another is already moving on, so the music never quite stops for breath.

Long-breathed lines

Melodies unfold in great spans with few resting points — a continuous, meditative unfolding.

Avoided cadences

Cadence points in the different voices are deliberately staggered, so a full stop rarely happens all at once.

Equal voices

No single part dominates; all are near-equal partners, weaving a dense, dark-hued web of sound.

Contemporaries prized this quality above showy display — a "sweet and seamless" richness later writers would struggle to describe.

04 — HOW THE TEXTURE WORKS

The Seamless Web

Picture the four voices as four ribbons. Each has its own phrases and its own cadences — but Ockeghem offsets them so that no two ribbons rest at the same moment. The joins are hidden; the sound is continuous.

How to read it: each coloured arc is one voice's phrase; a dot marks where that voice cadences. Because the dots are scattered left-to-right, one voice is always singing through another's rest — the essence of Ockeghem's continuous texture.

05 — THE PUZZLE-MAKER

The Art of Canon

A canon is a piece in which one voice imitates another exactly, note for note, after a delay — the principle of a round like Frère Jacques, raised to the level of high art. Ockeghem was its unrivalled master, delighting in canons written as riddles that the singers had to solve.

Strict canon

A second voice enters later and sings precisely the same melody — the two lines weaving as they overlap.

Mensuration canon

One written line read at the same time under different mensuration signs, so the voices move at different speeds.

Puzzle (riddle) canon

Only one line is written down, with a cryptic motto telling the singers how — and how many times — to derive the rest.

The Latin word canon itself means "rule": the instruction that unlocks the hidden voices from the single notated line.

06 — THE MASTERWORK

Missa prolationum

The Missa prolationum ("Mass of prolations") is one of the marvels of Western music. Every movement is built entirely from mensuration canons: just two written voices, each read under two different mensuration signs, generate four sounding parts — two pairs of canons unfolding at once, at ever-widening intervals. It is intellectual architecture that also sounds sublime.

The same melody in two voices: the follower enters after the leader and the two identical lines weave together — synthesised voices, pure Pythagorean tuning

Prolation is the medieval term for how a beat divides — into two or into three. Set the same notes under different prolations and one line becomes two at different speeds: the seed of the whole Mass.

07 — READING THE NOTATION

How to Read a Mensuration Canon

Only one line of notes is written. At its start sit two mensuration signs — and each singer reads the very same notes, but at the speed his sign dictates. One line becomes two voices moving apart.

How to read it: the two circles are mensuration signs — a full circle (◯) means the beat divides in three (perfect, slower); a broken circle (⎄, the ancestor of our "C") means it divides in two (imperfect, faster). Both singers read the identical notes, but Voice II moves quicker and steadily pulls ahead, so one written line sounds as two independent voices.

08 — ANOTHER RIDDLE

Missa cuiusvis toni — the "Mass in Any Mode"

Ockeghem's Missa cuiusvis toni is another feat of ingenuity. It is written without a fixed clef: the singers choose which clef to place at the front, and the very same notes then sound in a different mode each time — the whole Mass shifting its colour and its finals depending on how it is read.

One notated piece, several valid performances. It is a supreme demonstration that, for Ockeghem, the written page was a puzzle-box: the rule a performer applied could unlock more than one piece of music from the same ink.

The title cuiusvis toni is Latin for "of whichever mode": the clef the singers agree upon decides the tonal world of the entire Mass.

09 — THE MASS FOR THE DEAD

The Earliest Polyphonic Requiem

Ockeghem's Requiem is the earliest surviving polyphonic setting of the Mass for the Dead. (An earlier one by Du Fay is documented but lost.) Around the ancient chant melodies he wraps his flowing polyphony, each movement growing organically from the plainsong — a solemn, dark-hued sound that founds a tradition running through Victoria, Mozart and beyond.

Chant at its heart

The old Gregorian Requiem melodies are held in the texture and paraphrased in the upper voices.

A founding model

The first of the great polyphonic Requiems — the ancestor of every setting composers have written since.

Shifting textures

Ockeghem varies the number of voices movement by movement, from spare duets to full, sombre four-part writing.

10 — THE LOW VOICE

The Voice of the Bass

Ockeghem was himself a renowned bass, and he prized the deep register that many of his contemporaries left thin. His music reaches unusually low, giving his textures a dark, cavernous foundation — and it loves to settle onto the hollow ring of a bare open fifth, with no third to sweeten it, the sound of a great stone church.

Two low voices descend into the depths and come to rest on a bare fifth (D + A, no third) — the hollow, ringing medieval cadence, in pure Pythagorean tuning

The "open fifth" final — a chord of only the root and the fifth above — is the characteristic close of medieval and early-Renaissance polyphony, austere and luminous at once.

11 — THE LEGENDARY FEAT

A Canon in Thirty-Six Voices

A canon Deo gratias in as many as thirty-six voices is attributed to Ockeghem — the ultimate expression of his canonic ambition. Whether or not every one of those parts was ever meant to sound at once, the very idea captures his reputation: the composer for whom no contrapuntal feat was impossible.

12 — THE BRIDGE OF A GENERATION

Between Du Fay and Josquin

Ockeghem stands at the exact hinge of Renaissance music. He inherited the sweet, radiant idiom of the Burgundian masters and passed the art of imitative polyphony on to the generation of Josquin, who would perfect the "point of imitation". He was the honoured elder statesman of the age.

What he inherited

From Du Fay and Binchois: the smooth consonance and cantus-firmus craft of the Burgundian school — the foundation on which he built.

What he passed on

To Josquin and his contemporaries: canon, pervasive imitation and long-range architecture — the tools of the High Renaissance.

The theorist Johannes Tinctoris, who dedicated his counterpoint treatise (1477) to Ockeghem, counted him among the very greatest composers then living.

13 — DÉPLORATION

Mourned by a Generation

The déploration — a lament for a dead composer — was itself an Ockeghem tradition: he had mourned the death of Binchois in such a piece. When Ockeghem himself died in 1497, the finest poets and musicians of Europe returned the tribute. Josquin des Prez set Jean Molinet's lament as Nymphes des bois / La déploration de Johannes Ockeghem, weeping for the man they called their "good father".

Ockeghem mourns Binchois

His own déploration honoured the great Burgundian songsmith — one master saluting another across the generations.

Josquin mourns Ockeghem

Josquin's Nymphes des bois names the leading composers as grieving "children", wrapping the Requiem chant into the texture.

That the age's greatest composer should be mourned by its rising genius says everything about the esteem Ockeghem commanded.

14 — LISTENING GUIDE

How to Listen to Ockeghem

  • Let go of the phrase: follow the unbroken flow rather than waiting for tidy cadences — the music is meant to feel endless.
  • Listen downward — savour the dark bass and the hollow ring of the open-fifth cadences.
  • In the canonic works, try to catch the follower chasing the leader with the same tune, slightly behind.
  • Notice how all four voices feel like equals, none merely accompanying — a democracy of lines.

15 — LEGACY

The Prince of Music

Ockeghem showed that counterpoint could be at once a rigorous intellectual game and a thing of profound beauty — that a hidden rule could unlock four voices from two, or thirty-six from one, without ever sacrificing the seamless flow of the sound. His canons and his continuous textures pointed the way to Josquin and the High Renaissance, and his Requiem opened a tradition that has never closed.

Canon perfected

He raised strict, mensuration and puzzle canon to the summit of the art, inspiring composers for centuries.

The Requiem tradition

The earliest surviving polyphonic Mass for the Dead — the root of every Requiem written since.

A bridge to Josquin

His imitative craft passed directly into the mainstream of Renaissance polyphony.

"Put on garments of mourning, Josquin, Brumel, Pierchon, Compère, and weep great tears from your eyes: you have lost your good father."

— Jean Molinet, lament on the death of Ockeghem, set by Josquin des Prez as Nymphes des bois (1497)
