Palestrina

c. 1525–1594 · The Sound of the Counter-Reformation

The serene, flawless vocal counterpoint that became the very model of how voices should move together — dissonance always prepared, always resolved, the words shining through.

01 — THE MAN

Who Was Palestrina?

Giovanni Pierluigi da Palestrina (c. 1525–1594) took his name from the small hill-town near Rome where he was born, the ancient Praeneste. He spent almost his whole life in the service of the great Roman basilicas, and by the time he died he was hailed as the supreme master of sacred polyphony — the composer whose name would come to mean counterpoint itself.

02 — THE MOMENT

Rome & the Counter-Reformation

Palestrina worked at the heart of the Counter-Reformation — the Catholic Church's sweeping response to the Protestant Reformation. In an age of religious upheaval, Rome sought a sacred music that was dignified, orderly and above all intelligible: the holy words were not to be lost in a tangle of voices.

His answer was a polyphony of extraordinary balance and calm — many independent voices woven together, yet so smoothly that the sung Latin remains clear. It sounded, to his contemporaries and to us, like the very voice of an ordered, luminous faith.

The church's wish list

Clarity — the words must be understood.

Purity — no secular or "lascivious" tunes hidden in sacred music.

Reverence — beauty in the service of devotion, not display.

Palestrina met all three without sacrificing an ounce of artistry.

The reforming Council of Trent (1545–1563) set the tone; Palestrina became, in retrospect, the composer who best embodied its ideals for church music.

03 — LEGEND vs FACT

The Council of Trent & a Famous Myth

A beloved story says that the Council of Trent was about to ban all polyphony from the Mass — until Palestrina proved, with a single perfect work, that complex counterpoint could set the words clearly. He is said to have "saved polyphony". It is a wonderful tale. It is also a myth.

The legend

Trent nearly outlawed polyphony; Palestrina's Missa Papae Marcelli single-handedly changed the cardinals' minds and rescued the whole art. Later dramatised in Pfitzner's 1917 opera Palestrina.

The history

Trent's 1562 session urged clarity and the removal of anything profane, but planned no outright ban. No single Mass "saved" polyphony. The romantic rescue story grew up long after the fact.

Why it stuck: the Mass really is a marvel of clarity, so it feels like the perfect answer to Trent — history and legend flatter each other.

04 — THE MASTERWORK

Missa Papae Marcelli

Legend aside, the Pope Marcellus Mass is real and sublime. Published in 1567 in his Second Book of Masses, it is named for Pope Marcellus II, who reigned for barely three weeks in 1555. Scored for six voices, it is celebrated precisely for the quality the reformers longed for: the sacred text is audible throughout.

Six voices

Superius, alto, two tenors and two basses — rich yet transparent, often moving in clear, declaimed blocks.

Words first

Homophonic passages let whole phrases of Latin be heard at once; imitation returns without ever clouding the sense.

Serene arch

Smooth, mostly stepwise lines rise to gentle peaks and fall back — the hallmark Palestrina calm.

05 — THE STYLE

The Palestrina Style

His music is governed by a handful of principles so consistent that they could be — and were — turned into rules. Together they produce that unmistakable smooth, floating sound.

Stepwise motion

Melodies move mostly by step; leaps are rare, and a leap is answered by a step in the opposite direction.

Controlled dissonance

Clashes appear only on weak beats or as prepared suspensions — never harsh, always resolved by step.

Balanced arches

Each line rises to a single peak and gently descends; the whole texture breathes as one.

Independent voices

Every part is singable and shapely in its own right, yet all combine into pure consonance.

This became the stile antico — the "old style" — a timeless ideal that composers would study for centuries after his death.

06 — THE HEART OF THE STYLE

Controlled Dissonance: the Suspension

The single most characteristic gesture in Palestrina is the suspension: one voice holds a note while another moves beneath it, creating a momentary dissonance — a gentle "leaning" tension — which then resolves downward by step into sweetness. Chained together, suspensions give his cadences their yearning, serene glow.

Listen for the tiny "sigh" as the held note leans and then settles — that suspended-and-resolved dissonance is the emotional engine of the whole style.

07 — HOW TO READ IT

Reading a 4–3 Suspension

The classic suspension has three steps, named for what they do. The label "4–3" describes the interval between the two voices: the upper voice sounds a fourth above the lower (a dissonance), then resolves to a third (a consonance).

1 · Prepare

The note that will clash is first sounded as a consonance — here a comfortable third. The ear accepts it.

2 · Suspend

The lower voice moves down; the upper voice is held over, so the same note is now a dissonant fourth — the tension.

3 · Resolve

The held voice steps down to the third. The dissonance melts into consonance — the sigh of relief.

08 — THE GOAL OF EVERY PHRASE

The Warm Full-Triad Cadence

Where medieval cadences often landed on hollow, open fifths, Palestrina closes on a full, sweet triad — root, third and fifth all sounding. A phrase of imitation gathers, a suspension leans, and the voices settle onto a rich major chord: the sound of the word "Amen".

Synthesised choir · a plagal "Amen" with a 4–3 suspension leaning into a glowing full G-major triad

Equal temperament lets the thirds ring pure and mellow — the sweetness that defines Renaissance harmony after the open sonorities of the Middle Ages.

09 — MELODY

The Smooth, Singing Line

Every Palestrina melody is designed to be sung with ease. It moves mostly by step, rises to a single high point, and curves gently back down — a balanced arch. Large leaps are used sparingly and always smoothed over by a step in the opposite direction. The famous opening of the motet Sicut cervus is the perfect example.

Synthesised voice · a stepwise line rising to a peak and gently falling — the shape of a Palestrina point of imitation

Each voice enters with this same shapely subject in turn ("imitation"), so the whole texture is woven from one graceful idea.

10 — THE OUTPUT

A Vast, Ordered Output

Palestrina's productivity was prodigious and almost entirely sacred — the fruit of a lifetime inside the Roman liturgy. His works were printed and treasured across Catholic Europe.

Over 100 Masses

More than any other composer of the age — built on chant, on his own motets, and on the famous L'homme armé and parody techniques.

Hundreds of motets

Including settings of the Song of Songs and the widely loved Sicut cervus ("As the deer longs for the water").

Hymns & Magnificats

Complete cycles of office hymns, Magnificats, offertories, lamentations — a working repertory for the whole church year.

11 — THE TEXTBOOK

Fux & the Rules of Counterpoint

Palestrina's practice was so consistent that, over a century after his death, the Austrian composer Johann Joseph Fux distilled it into a teaching method: Gradus ad Parnassum ("Steps to Parnassus", 1725). Written as a dialogue, its wise master is named Aloysius — the Latin for Luigi — an open homage to Palestrina.

Fux's system of species counterpoint — adding voices in graded stages of complexity — is still taught today. It shaped Haydn, Mozart and Beethoven, all of whom studied from it. Through Fux, the Palestrina style became the grammar of Western music.

The five species

1st — note against note

2nd — two notes against one

3rd — four notes against one

4th — syncopation & suspensions

5th — florid: all combined

The suspension you heard earlier is the very heart of Fux's fourth species — Palestrina's leaning dissonance, made into a lesson.

12 — THE OLD STYLE

The Stile Antico — the Immortal Model

Within a generation of Palestrina's death, music changed radically: Monteverdi's expressive seconda pratica unleashed dramatic dissonance, and the Baroque was born. But Palestrina's manner did not die — it became the revered prima pratica or stile antico, the "old style" deliberately kept alive for sacred music.

A living reference

Composers switched into the "old style" for church works long after it had ceased to be modern — a mark of gravity and reverence.

Bach & Mozart

Both studied Palestrina's counterpoint; Bach copied out and performed his music, and echoes of the stile antico run through their sacred works.

The Cecilian revival

In the 19th century a movement took Palestrina as the ideal of pure church music, restoring and imitating his style anew.

Few composers become a permanent "style" that later ages consciously return to. Palestrina is one of the very few.

14 — LISTENING GUIDE

How to Listen to Palestrina

  • Follow one voice at a time — hear how singable and shapely each line is on its own.
  • Wait for the suspensions at cadences: the held note that leans, then resolves down with a sigh.
  • Notice the balance — no voice dominates; the texture floats, weightless and serene.
  • Listen for the words: even in six parts, the Latin stays remarkably clear.

15 — LEGACY

The Prince of Music

More than any composer before him, Palestrina turned polyphony into a serene, self-contained perfection — and then, through Fux and the stile antico, that perfection became the grammar every later composer learned. To study counterpoint at all is, still today, to study Palestrina. His calm remains, four centuries on, the very sound of ordered beauty.

The model

His style, codified by Fux, is the foundation of counterpoint teaching from Bach's day to the conservatoire of today.

The sound

Smooth lines, prepared dissonance and full triads define, for most listeners, the very sound of "Renaissance church music".

The ideal

For the Cecilian reformers and countless choirs since, Palestrina is the enduring emblem of pure, prayerful music.
