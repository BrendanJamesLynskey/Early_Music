William Byrd

c. 1540 – 1623 · The English Renaissance

A recusant Catholic in a Protestant realm, he wrote sublime music for two irreconcilable churches — and all but invented the English art of writing for the keyboard.

History of Early Music · The Tudor Master

01 — INTRODUCTION

Who Was William Byrd?

The greatest English composer of his age — pupil of Thomas Tallis, organist of the Chapel Royal, and the towering figure of the Elizabethan and Jacobean musical world. He was master of every form his century knew: Latin polyphony, English anthems, songs, viol consorts and, above all, the keyboard.

03 — THE CENTRAL PARADOX

Master of Two Worlds

Byrd's genius was split, of necessity, between two hostile churches. He wrote glorious Latin music for the outlawed Catholic Mass and, at the same time, dignified English music for the Protestant liturgy he was employed to serve. He excelled at both.

✝ The Old Faith (Latin)

For clandestine Catholic worship in the houses of the gentry: the three Latin Masses (for three, four and five voices) and the two great books of Gradualia — motets for the Church year.

♛ The Queen's Church (English)

For the Chapel Royal and the Anglican rite: the magnificent Great Service, verse and full anthems, and settings of the English liturgy.

That one man could serve both worlds so completely is the abiding wonder of his career — and a measure of how highly he was protected.

04 — FAITH & RISK

A Recusant's Courage

A recusant was one who refused to attend the services of the established Church — a criminal offence in Elizabethan England, punishable by ruinous fines and worse. Byrd and his family were repeatedly cited for recusancy, their names on the court rolls for years. Yet he was never destroyed: the Queen's own regard, and powerful Catholic friends, shielded her finest musician.

The danger

Priests were hunted and executed; hearing Mass was treasonous. To print Latin Mass music was audacious.

The protection

His standing at court and among the recusant nobility kept him at liberty and composing.

The conviction

His secret sacred works are charged with a personal urgency — the music of a persecuted faith.

His Catholic Masses were printed c. 1592–1595 with no title page and no publisher's name — discreet enough to be denied, defiant enough to exist at all.

05 — THE SECRET CATHOLIC · I

The Three Latin Masses

Byrd's supreme act of devotion is his set of three Masses — for three, four and five voices, printed in the 1590s for singing in secret, intimate gatherings. They are compact, intensely expressive and perfectly wrought: dense imitative polyphony flowering into radiant, full-voiced cadences on the holiest words.

Mass for Three Voices

The most intimate — three lines only, ideal for a handful of singers around a table.

Mass for Four Voices

The best known: taut, poignant, its "Agnus Dei" one of the great pages of Tudor music.

Mass for Five Voices

The grandest and most sonorous of the three, rich in overlapping suspensions.

06 — THE SECRET CATHOLIC · II

Gradualia & the Jewel of Ave verum corpus

Beyond the Masses, Byrd set out to provide music for the entire Catholic Church year. The two books of the Gradualia (1605, 1607) gather motets for the Proper of the Mass — feasts, votive observances, the great seasons — a monumental, systematic act of liturgical love.

Its most beloved page is Ave verum corpus — a short, hushed motet whose gently aching dissonances on "miserere mei" ("have mercy on me") distil a whole persecuted faith into a few unforgettable bars. It remains one of the best-loved pieces in all choral music.

07 — THE ANGLICAN BYRD

For the Queen's Church

As a paid servant of the Chapel Royal, Byrd also gave the new Protestant liturgy some of its noblest music — proving that his art could clothe English words as splendidly as Latin.

The Great Service

His most ambitious English work: a full setting of the Anglican canticles for the divided choir of a great cathedral — spacious, sonorous, magnificent.

The Anthems

Both the full anthem (all voices together) and the newer verse anthem (solo voices alternating with choir and instruments), which he helped shape into an English art form.

♪ Listen: Byrd — The Great Service

The English verse anthem, with its dialogue of soloist and chorus, points forward to the whole later tradition of English cathedral music — and to Purcell.

08 — THE KEYBOARD MASTER

Father of the English Virginalists

Byrd founded the English virginalist school — the first great flowering of solo keyboard music anywhere in Europe. The virginals was a small plucked keyboard instrument (a kind of table harpsichord), beloved in Tudor households. Byrd wrote pavans and galliards, dance-pairs, fantasias, and dazzling sets of variations for it.

09 — NOTATION & FORM

Division on a Ground

A favourite virginalist form. A short bass line — the ground — repeats over and over, while the right hand spins ever busier divisions (variations, literally the "division" of long notes into quick ones) above it. Below: the ground on the lower staff loops; the upper line grows florid.

How to read it: read left→right. The lower staff (F-clef) plays the same four bass notes again and again — that fixed loop is the ground. Above it (G-clef) the melody is different every time round, breaking into faster and faster notes: each pass is a "division". Higher on the staff = higher pitch; there are no bar-lines to slow the eye.

10 — HOW TO READ IT

Reading Keyboard Notation

Keyboard music needs two staves at once, braced together into a "grand staff" — one for each hand. This two-hand layout, which Byrd's generation helped standardise, is still exactly how piano music is written today.

1 · Two staves

The top staff is usually the right hand, the lower the left. They are read at the same moment, top and bottom aligned.

2 · Two clefs

Treble (G-clef) for the higher hand, bass (F-clef) for the lower. Each names the pitches on its own staff.

3 · Middle C

The two staves meet at middle C, which floats on a short ledger line between them — the hinge of the keyboard.

4 · Chords

Notes stacked vertically sound together; a hand can strike several keys at once, so Byrd's textures are rich.

11 — SONGS & CONSORT

Songs, Viols & the 1588 Songbook

Byrd was also the great English pioneer of secular and domestic music. His consort songs — a solo voice weaving through a consort of viols — are a distinctively English genre he did much to perfect. In 1588 he published Psalmes, Sonets & Songs of Sadnes and Pietie, a landmark printed collection for voices.

The consort song

A single expressive voice supported by a quartet or quintet of viols — private, intimate music for the Elizabethan household.

In the preface to the 1588 songbook Byrd set out his famous "Reasons briefly set down… to persuade every one to learn to sing", extolling music's power to gladden and to heal — one of the earliest English arguments for musical education. He championed, above all, the human voice.

"The exercise of singing is delightful to Nature, and good to preserve the health of Man."

— William Byrd, Psalmes, Sonets & Songs (1588)
12 — MASTER & PUPIL

Tallis, Byrd & the Printing Monopoly

In 1575 Elizabeth I granted Thomas Tallis and his pupil Byrd an extraordinary privilege: a monopoly on printing music and ruled music-paper in England. Together they marked it with the Cantiones Sacrae of that year — Latin motets dedicated to the Queen. It was a business venture as much as an honour, and it bound master and pupil for life.

The privilege

Sole right to print polyphonic music and music-paper — a rare royal favour, and a commercial gamble.

Cantiones Sacrae 1575

Their joint publication of Latin motets, offered to Elizabeth I, launched the enterprise.

The bond

When Tallis died in 1585, Byrd mourned him in the elegy Ye sacred Muses — "Tallis is dead, and Music dies."

♪ Listen: Byrd — Ye sacred Muses (elegy for Tallis)

The venture lost money at first — English demand for printed part-music was small — but it made Byrd the dominant published composer of his age.

13 — THE CRAFT

Words First, Then Notes

What unites Byrd's Latin motets, English anthems and keyboard grounds is a rigorous, deeply serious craft: supple imitative counterpoint, harmony of great expressive weight, and above all a conviction that music must grow from the meaning of its words. He wrote that the very sense of a sacred text seemed to summon the right notes of its own accord.

"There is a certain hidden power in the thoughts underlying the words themselves; so that, as one meditates upon the sacred words and constantly and seriously considers them, the right notes, in some inexplicable fashion, suggest themselves quite spontaneously."

— William Byrd, preface to Gradualia (1605)

This word-driven ideal aligns Byrd with the great Continental masters — Lassus, Victoria, Palestrina — while his voice remains unmistakably English.

14 — LISTENING GUIDE

How to Listen to Byrd

  • In the Masses, follow how each phrase of text enters voice by voice, then blooms into a full-voiced cadence at the holiest words.
  • In Ave verum corpus, listen for the tender clashes on "miserere mei" — dissonance used as pure feeling.
  • In the keyboard music, catch the repeating ground bass and hear the right hand grow busier with each variation.
  • In the verse anthems, enjoy the dialogue of solo voices answered by the full choir.

15 — LEGACY

A Father of Musick

Byrd stands as the summit of English music before Purcell. He gave the verse anthem and the English keyboard tradition their classic shape, wrote sacred music of a depth rivalling any on the Continent, and did it all while quietly keeping a forbidden faith. His pupils and the virginalists after him — Bull, Gibbons, Tomkins — built directly on his foundation.

Sacred polyphony

The three Masses and the Gradualia are the crown of English Catholic music — sung the world over today.

The keyboard

The pavans, grounds and variations founded a school of keyboard writing that leads on to Bull, Gibbons and beyond.

The anthem

His English church music shaped a tradition that runs unbroken to Purcell and the modern cathedral repertory.

"In mourning weeds, with tears in eyes: Tallis is dead, and Music dies."

— William Byrd, Ye sacred Muses, his elegy for Thomas Tallis (1585)
