Troubadours, Trouvères & Minnesänger

c. 1100–1300 · The Rise of Named Secular Song

The first great flowering of song in the everyday tongue — monophonic melodies of fin’amor, courtly love, sung by named aristocratic poet-composers across mediaeval France and Germany.

History of Early Music · No. 03 — The Voice of Courtly Love

01 — DEFINITION

Song Steps Out of the Church

For centuries the written melody had belonged to the Latin liturgy. Now, from about 1100, aristocratic poet-musicians began to compose in the vernacular — and, for the first time, to attach their names to what they made. The subject was not God but earthly, idealised love: fin’amor.

♪

Monophonic

A single melodic line, like chant — the manuscripts preserve only the tune, no written harmony.

📜

Vernacular

Not Latin but the spoken tongue — Occitan in the south, Old French in the north, Middle High German in the Empire.

❤

Fin’amor

“Refined love”: the poet serves a distant, idealised lady, ennobled by longing and devotion.

⚑

Named & noble

Sung by dukes, countesses and knights — composers we can name, unlike anonymous chant.

These are the ancestors of every later art-song: the moment when the individual voice, and the individual composer, enter secular music.

02 — THREE TRADITIONS

South, North & the Empire

One courtly ideal, three regions and three tongues. The art was born in the sunlit south of France, travelled north, and was taken up by the German-speaking lands.

The word troubadour comes from Occitan trobar, “to find, to compose”; the German Minne means courtly love — hence Minnesänger, “singers of love”.

03 — TWO CENTURIES

From Aquitaine to Arras

Roughly two hundred years separate the first troubadour from the last great trouvère — an art carried across Europe by crusade, marriage and the wandering of singers.

The Albigensian Crusade (1209 onward) devastated the courts of Occitania, scattering the troubadours and hastening the art’s move north and east.

04 — THE TROUBADOURS

The Poets of the South

In the courts of Occitania the art was born. Its makers ranged from ruling dukes to a woman of the nobility — each shaping the language of longing that all later love-song would inherit.

Guillaume IX of Aquitaine · 1071–1126

Duke of Aquitaine and grandfather of Eleanor of Aquitaine — the first troubadour whose work survives, worldly and bawdy by turns.

Bernart de Ventadorn · fl. c.1147–70

Of humble birth but the greatest melodist of them all; his songs of pure devotion set the model for the classic canso.

Comtessa de Dia (Beatriz)

A trobairitz — a woman troubadour — whose A chantar is the only surviving trobairitz song to keep its music.

Jaufré Rudel

The poet of amor de lonh, “distant love” — famed for loving from afar a lady he had never seen.

Occitan was the polished literary language of the Mediterranean south; its poetry set a European fashion imitated for generations.

05 — THE CANSO

Bernart de Ventadorn & the Love Song

The canso — the great love song — was the troubadour’s central art. Bernart’s Can vei la lauzeta mover (“When I see the lark move”) opens with the poet watching a lark beat its wings for joy in the sunlight, then falling into his own sorrow. Its melody is a gently arching line, rising to a peak and settling back to rest.

♪ Listen: Can vei la lauzeta (recording)

A stepwise, arching love-melody in the style of Bernart — synthesised single voice, free rhythm, pure fifths

“Can vei la lauzeta mover / de joi sas alas contra’l rai” — When I see the lark beat its wings for joy against the sun’s ray…

— Bernart de Ventadorn, opening of his most celebrated canso
06 — THE TROBAIRITZ

The Women Troubadours

Not all troubadours were men. The trobairitz — noblewomen who composed their own cansos — answered the courtly-love convention from the other side, writing with a directness that still startles.

The best known is the Comtessa de Dia (often called Beatriz). Her song A chantar holds a unique place: it is the only surviving trobairitz song that keeps its music as well as its words. In it she sings, with wounded pride, of a lover who has spurned her.

♪ Listen: Comtessa de Dia — A chantar (recording)

Fewer than two dozen trobairitz are known by name; that only one of their songs survives with music makes A chantar uniquely precious.

07 — THE TROUVÈRES

The Art Travels North

In the north of France the courtly song was taken up in the langue d’oïl — the ancestor of modern French — by the trouvères. Their name is simply the northern form of “troubadour”.

Adam de la Halle · c.1245–1288

The last and most versatile of the great trouvères, working in Arras. His Le Jeu de Robin et Marion is an early secular play with songs — a distant ancestor of the musical stage.

Thibaut IV of Champagne

Count of Champagne and King of Navarre — a ruling monarch who was also a gifted and prolific trouvère, showing how deeply song ran through courtly life.

Because northern courts were wealthier patrons of manuscript-making, far more trouvère melodies survive than troubadour ones.

08 — THE MINNESÄNGER

Courtly Love in the German Lands

The same ideal took root in the German-speaking Empire, where its singers were the Minnesänger — from Minne, courtly love. They sang of devotion, of nature and the turning seasons, and sometimes of the world’s affairs.

Walther von der Vogelweide · c.1170–1230

The greatest of the Minnesänger — celebrated for his poetic range, from tender love-songs to sharp verses on politics and morality. A towering figure of mediaeval German literature.

One European art

Troubadour, trouvère and Minnesänger were branches of a single courtly culture — the ideal of refined love crossing languages and borders across two centuries.

Later German tradition remembered this age in the legendary song-contest at the Wartburg, dramatised centuries afterwards by Wagner in Tannhäuser.

09 — THE GENRES

Kinds of Song

The troubadours named and refined a whole family of song-types, each with its own subject and mood. Four of the most important:

Canso

The love song — the central, most prestigious genre, on the theme of fin’amor.

Sirventes

A political or satirical song — moral complaint, praise or attack, often to a borrowed tune.

Alba

The “dawn song”: lovers must part as day breaks, often warned by a watchman.

Estampie

A dance — one of the very earliest surviving instrumental forms, purely melodic and lively.

Most of these were sung; the estampie stands apart as instrumental dance music — a rare survival of mediaeval secular instrumental repertory.

10 — THE DANCE

The Estampie — Music to Move To

Among the very few mediaeval instrumental pieces to survive is the estampie, a brisk dance built from repeated phrases (puncta), each played twice with a different ending. Where the love songs were sung to a lady, the estampie set the whole court on its feet.

11 — READING THE PAGE

One Line, Many Rhythms

Troubadour and trouvère songs were written down much like chant: a single melodic line of neumes on a staff, giving the pitches but — crucially — no clear rhythm. The performer must decide how long each note lasts.

1 · Pitch is given

Read the neumes off the staff exactly as in chant: the clef names one line, higher on the page means higher in pitch.

2 · Rhythm is not

The notation records no durations. The very same line can be sung in a smooth even flow or a springing long–short dance lilt.

3 · The performer chooses

Modern singers must interpret — guided by the words, the genre and taste. Two fine recordings of one song may sound utterly different.

12 — IN PERFORMANCE

Voice, Vielle & Harp

A troubadour rarely sang entirely alone. Manuscript illuminations and poems describe accompaniment on stringed instruments — yet the sources notate only the melody, leaving the rest to the player’s art.

🎻

The vielle

The bowed mediaeval fiddle — the favoured instrument of the courtly singer, able to double or answer the voice.

🎹

Plucked strings

The harp and early plucked instruments carried melodies and dance-tunes; the lute would rise to prominence a little later.

✎

Only the tune survives

Because manuscripts give a bare line, all accompaniment, drones and ornament in a modern performance are reconstruction.

This is why two ensembles can realise the same song so differently — everything but the melody itself is an informed act of imagination.

13 — LISTENING GUIDE

How to Listen to Courtly Song

  • Follow the words — the melody exists to carry a poem of longing; feel how the tune rises with the emotion of the verse.
  • Notice each ensemble’s rhythmic choice — even and flowing, or a dancing long–short lilt: the notation leaves it open.
  • Hear the accompaniment as invention — drones, vielle and harp are the performers’ reconstruction, not the manuscript.
  • Compare a canso and an estampie — the same courtly world in its tender and its dancing moods.

14 — LEGACY

Where All Love-Song Begins

The troubadours and their heirs invented something that never left Western music: the named composer singing, in his own tongue, of earthly love. The ideal of fin’amor shaped European poetry for centuries; the melodic craft of the canso fed into the later art of the chanson and, in time, the whole tradition of song.

The named composer

For the first time secular music has authors — dukes, countesses and knights whose songs we can name and sing.

The vernacular

Song in the everyday tongue, on themes of love and the world — the seed of every later art-song and chanson.

The courtly ideal

The code of refined love that shaped mediaeval and Renaissance poetry, and echoes in love-song to this day.

“Chantars no pot gaire valer, si d’ins dal cor no mou lo chans” — Singing can be worth little if the song does not move from within the heart.

— Bernart de Ventadorn, opening of a canso
