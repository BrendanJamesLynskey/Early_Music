Hildegard von Bingen

1098–1179 · Benedictine Abbess, Visionary & Composer

The first composer whose life and works we can name and read — a Rhineland nun whose ecstatic, soaring chant leaps across two octaves where anonymous plainchant merely steps, sung to words of light and greenness that were entirely her own.

History of Early Music · No. 02 — A Named Voice

01 — THE POLYMATH

Who Was Hildegard?

Born in 1098 in the German Rhineland and given to the Church as a child, Hildegard became a Benedictine abbess and one of the most extraordinary minds of the twelfth century — a woman of many callings who, remarkably for her age, put her name to a body of music that still survives.

👁

Visionary

From childhood she saw radiant visions of "the Living Light", which she recorded in words and images.

✎

Theologian & poet

She wrote her own dense, luminous Latin texts, sermons and letters read across Europe.

🌿

Healer

She studied the natural world, plants and the body, writing on medicine and healing.

♪

Composer

One of the first composers with a named, surviving body of work — her Symphonia.

Anonymous chant had no author; with Hildegard, for the first time, we can attach music this individual to a single named creator.

02 — A LIFE

A Life on the Rhine

Hildegard's long life spanned almost the whole of the twelfth century. She rose from an enclosed child-oblate to the founder and leader of her own communities, writing, composing and corresponding to the end.

03 — THE VISIONS

The Living Light

From earliest childhood Hildegard experienced luminous visions — not, she insisted, in dreams or ecstasy, but with her ordinary waking senses, a brightness she called the Living Light. In middle age she was commanded, as she understood it, to write them down.

Out of that command flowed everything else: her theological books, her natural science, her letters — and her music, which she heard as the sound of heaven breaking into the world. Her songs are the visions made audible.

Hildegard described herself, with humility, as an untaught vessel — "a feather on the breath of God", carried aloft not by her own strength but by the wind of the divine.

04 — VIRIDITAS

Viriditas — the Greening Power

Her most personal idea is viriditas — "greenness" or the greening life-force. For Hildegard, the sap that swells a bud, the vitality of a healthy body and the freshness of a soul touched by grace are all one divine energy. Her lyrics glow with it, and with images of light, dawn, and the Virgin.

Greenness

Nature's vigour and moist freshness — a favourite word, viriditas, running through her poetry as a sign of God's living power.

Light

Radiance, fire and dawn: the vocabulary of her visions carried straight into the imagery of her sung texts.

The Virgin

Many songs honour Mary as the "greenest branch" — the flowering shoot from which new life springs.

She calls the Virgin O viridissima virga — "O greenest branch" — fusing her love of green growing things with her Marian devotion.

05 — HER SONGS

The Symphonia

Hildegard gathered her chants into a collection whose title itself is a vision: Symphonia armonie celestium revelationum — the "Symphony of the Harmony of Celestial Revelations". These are her own words set to her own melodies: antiphons, responsories, sequences and hymns.

Antiphons & responsories

Short, intense chants framing the psalms of the Divine Office, such as the antiphon O frondens virga.

Sequences & hymns

Longer, soaring pieces for great feasts — the sequence O virga ac diadema, the hymn-like Ave generosa.

Still monophonic plainchant — a single unaccompanied line, in the tradition she inherited — yet utterly unlike anonymous chant in its restless, personal shape.

06 — MELODIC STYLE

A New Kind of Chant

Where ordinary chant moves gently, mostly by step within a narrow band, Hildegard's melodies soar and plunge. This is what makes her voice instantly recognisable.

↕

Enormous range

A single chant can span more than two octaves — vast for its time.

↗

Dramatic leaps

Bold jumps of a fifth or a whole octave, where anonymous chant would only step.

∞

Long melismas

Florid cascades of many notes flowering on a single syllable.

☉

Modes used freely

She roams within the church modes with an ecstatic freedom all her own.

The effect is rapturous rather than restful — melody as an act of longing, straining upward toward the light of her visions.

07 — HEAR IT

The Arching Phrase

Listen for the fingerprint of her style: a phrase that springs up through a wide leap, curls around a high peak in a florid melisma, then sinks gently back to rest on the final E. Anonymous chant almost never dares this.

♪ Listen: O virga ac diadema (Sequentia)

Synthesised single voice · pure Pythagorean tuning · free rhythm — note the upward leap and the turning melisma at the peak

The wide interval near the start and the ornamented curve around the top are exactly the gestures that set her chant apart from the anonymous repertory.

08 — READING THE PAGE

Reading Her Neumes

Hildegard's chant survives in early staff notation: square neumes on a four-line staff, the same kind of page used for anonymous chant. But the shapes on her page behave very differently — see the wide leap and the long melisma.

How to read it: read left to right; higher on the staff = higher in pitch. The clef names one line, and every note is read relative to it. A cluster of squares over one syllable is a melisma — read a spread group left-to-right, a stacked group bottom-to-top. There is no written rhythm: the notes are broadly equal and shaped by the words. What marks this as Hildegard is the sudden leap up and the exceptionally long melisma.

A luminous line rising to a high peak, blossoming into a melisma, and falling back to the final E

09 — SACRED DRAMA

Ordo Virtutum

Around 1151 Hildegard wrote the Ordo Virtutum ("Play of the Virtues") — a sung morality play in which the Virtues, allegorical figures with named roles, contend with the Devil for a human Soul. It is arguably the earliest surviving liturgical drama, an ancestor of opera itself.

The singing Virtues

The Soul and the Virtues sing throughout, in Hildegard's own soaring, melismatic chant — music is the very substance of the drama.

The Devil who cannot sing

The Devil alone has no melody: he can only shout and speak. To be cut off from song is, for Hildegard, to be cut off from God.

♪ Listen: Ordo Virtutum (recording)

That the wicked character is denied music is one of the earliest examples of dramatic characterisation through sound.

10 — HER TEXTS

Words of Light

Unlike most chant composers, who set the fixed words of the liturgy, Hildegard wrote her own poetry — an idiosyncratic, image-drenched Latin unlike anyone else's. Reading her titles is to step inside her visionary world.

O viridissima virga

"O greenest branch" — the Virgin as the freshest green shoot of new life.

O ignis Spiritus

"O fire of the Spirit" — the Holy Spirit as living flame and light.

O virga ac diadema

"O branch and diadem" — a great sequence weaving her images of branch, crown and purity.

Columba aspexit

A luminous sequence, its melody blossoming into long ecstatic melismas.

Ave generosa

"Hail, noble one" — a radiant, tender hymn of praise to the Virgin.

O frondens virga

"O leafing branch" — a short antiphon, again green and growing.

Notice how many titles begin "O" and turn on the same charged images — branch, green, fire, light — the private symbol-language of her visions.

12 — HER WORLD

Rupertsberg & Eibingen

Hildegard did not only write and compose — she built. Around mid-century she led her sisters out of their older community to found a convent of her own at Rupertsberg, near Bingen on the Rhine, and later established a second house across the river at Eibingen.

Rupertsberg

The convent she founded and led as abbess, where her sisters sang her chant and where she died in 1179.

Eibingen

Her second foundation on the opposite bank of the Rhine — her name is still honoured there today.

Her music was not written for concert halls but for the daily round of prayer sung by the nuns of her own communities.

13 — LISTENING GUIDE

How to Listen to Hildegard

  • Follow the leaps — her line springs upward by fifths and octaves where ordinary chant would only step.
  • Wait for the melismas to bloom — long cascades of notes on a single syllable at the words she loves most.
  • Feel the arch — most phrases rise to an ecstatic peak and then sink back to the final.
  • Let go of the beat: like all chant, this is free rhythm, shaped by her Latin, with no pulse to tap.

14 — LEGACY

A Feather on the Breath of God

For centuries Hildegard was remembered as a saint and seer; only in modern times has she been recognised as one of the great composers of the Middle Ages. In 2012 she was canonised and named a Doctor of the Church — a rare honour affirming the depth of her thought. Her ecstatic, soaring line stands utterly apart: the first music we can hear as the work of a single, named, unmistakable voice.

A named voice

One of the first composers with a substantial surviving body of work we can attribute by name.

Ecstatic chant

Monophony pushed to the edge of rapture — enormous range, bold leaps, long florid melismas.

Doctor of the Church

Declared a Doctor of the Church in 2012 — theologian, poet, healer and musician in one.

"I am a feather on the breath of God."
