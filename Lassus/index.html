Orlando di Lasso

1532–1594 · The Cosmopolitan Genius of the Late Renaissance

The most versatile and prolific master of the sixteenth century — over two thousand works in every genre and tongue of Europe — matching music to word and mood with wit, drama and a daring, tear-bright chromaticism.

History of Early Music · No. 13 — The Late Renaissance

01 — INTRODUCTION

Who Was Orlando di Lasso?

Known by as many names as the lands he served — Roland de Lassus in French, Orlando di Lasso in Italian, Orlandus Lassus in the Latin of his prints — he was the towering all-rounder of the late Renaissance. Where his contemporary Palestrina cultivated one serene ideal, Lassus poured himself into every style, language and mood the age could offer.

Prolific

Over 2,000 surviving works — motets, Masses, madrigals, chansons and Lieder — one of the largest outputs of any Renaissance composer.

✈

Cosmopolitan

Franco-Flemish by birth, Italian by training, Bavarian by service — fluent in the music of four nations.

✦

Dramatic

Vivid and pictorial, he bent every device — rhythm, colour, chromaticism — to paint the meaning of his texts.

♛

Celebrated

The most famous musician of his day, sought by popes and princes, and honoured across the courts of Europe.

Franco-Flemish composers dominated European music for a century; Lassus was the last and most universal of the great line.

02 — A LIFE ACROSS EUROPE

From Mons to Munich

A childhood voice so beautiful that, tradition holds, he was kidnapped three times for it carried the young Roland out of his native Hainaut and across the courts of Italy — before he settled for forty years in Bavaria as the most honoured musician in Europe.

03 — THE UNIVERSAL VOICE

Master of Every Tongue

No composer of the century ranged so freely across languages and genres. Lassus wrote as convincingly a solemn Latin motet as a bawdy Italian villanella or a witty French chanson — each idiom caught to the life.

Latin — sacred

Hundreds of motets and dozens of Masses: the grand liturgical repertory that made his European name.

Italian — madrigals

From polished madrigals to earthy villanelle, learnt first-hand in Naples and Rome.

French — chansons

Elegant and often comic chansons, quick-witted in their word-setting and rhythm.

German — Lieder

Sturdy polyphonic Lieder for his Bavarian patrons and the German-speaking market.

Every mood

Devotion, grief, laughter, satire — he commanded the whole emotional gamut of the Renaissance.

Every scale

From an intimate three-voice song to vast polychoral splendour for great occasions.

This universality is his signature: contemporaries called such a composer divino — the "divine Orlando".

04 — THE CHROMATIC PROLOGUE

Prophetiae Sibyllarum

His most radical experiment. In the Prophetiae Sibyllarum — settings of the prophecies of the pagan Sibyls foretelling Christ — the famous prologue announces its own strangeness: "these songs you hear are modulated in a chromatic strain". The harmony lurches through keys and accidentals with a boldness that would not be matched for centuries.

A chromatically rising top line over restlessly shifting triads — equal temperament, in the spirit of the prologue

The prologue's Latin verse literally describes its own technique — a manifesto in music for the "chromatic" style then fascinating Italian theorists.

05 — HOW TO READ IT

Chromatic vs Diatonic

The heart of Lassus's daring is chromaticism — melody that moves by semitones, the smallest step, calling in notes outside the plain scale by writing accidentals (♯ sharp, ♭ flat). Set it against the diatonic norm — the seven "white-note" steps of the mode — and the difference is plain to see and hear.

How to read it: a sharp (♯) raises the next note a semitone, a flat (♭) lowers it. Where the diatonic line skips, the chromatic line creeps — every step half the size.

06 — MUSIC PAINTS THE WORD

Wit, Colour & Word-Painting

Lassus is the supreme pictorialist: he makes the notes do what the words say. A phrase about laughter breaks into quick, chattering patter; a sigh droops chromatically; a rising hope climbs the stave; a word for depth plunges the bass. In his lighter madrigals and chansons this becomes pure comic delight.

Running words

Fast, repeated syllables set to rapid equal notes — the voices tumble over one another like speech in a hurry.

Falling grief

Sorrow and death drawn as drooping, chromatic descents — the sound leans downward with the sense.

Rising joy

Praise, heaven and hope lifted by ascending lines and bright, open sonorities.

07 — THE GREAT SACRED CYCLE

The Penitential Psalms

For his patron Duke Albrecht V of Bavaria, Lassus composed the Psalmi Davidis poenitentiales — settings of the seven Penitential Psalms of David, the traditional prayers of repentance.

It is one of the monuments of Renaissance sacred music: grave, deeply expressive, and copied into two sumptuous illuminated volumes kept as a treasure of the Munich court. Here Lassus turns his pictorial gift inward, painting sin, sorrow and the longing for mercy.

Why "penitential"?

Seven psalms — including the great Miserere — long grouped by the Church as prayers of contrition.

Expressive means: shifting textures, dark low sonorities and telling dissonance to mirror the pleading text.

08 — THE MOTETS

The Latin Motets

The core of his fame rests on the motets — hundreds of them, gathered in life and posthumously in the great collection his sons published as the Magnum opus musicum. They range from serene devotion to raw, trembling drama.

Timor et tremor

"Fear and trembling" — a celebrated motet of anguished supplication, its dissonances and shifting harmony making the terror audible. A model of Lassus's dramatic sacred manner.

Music for the word

Each phrase of text draws its own musical gesture — clarity of declamation wedded to bold expressive colour.

09 — SONGS OF THREE NATIONS

Madrigals, Chansons & Lieder

Alongside the sacred grandeur ran a torrent of secular song — and here Lassus is at his most human: tender, satirical, tipsy, heartbroken by turns. The same hand that wrote the Penitential Psalms wrote riotous drinking songs.

Italian madrigals & villanelle

Refined settings of love-poetry beside earthy, quick-fire comic songs picked up in Naples.

French chansons

Sparkling, sharply characterised — from courtly grace to broad Rabelaisian humour.

German Lieder

Sturdy, singable part-songs that helped shape the German secular tradition.

This effortless code-switching between languages and moods is exactly what "cosmopolitan" means in his art.

10 — THE LAST TESTAMENT

Lagrime di San Pietro (1594)

His final work, finished only weeks before his death and dedicated to the Pope: the Lagrime di San Pietro — "The Tears of St Peter" — a cycle of madrigals on the apostle's remorse after denying Christ. Ageing and unwell, Lassus poured a lifetime's expressive mastery into this profound meditation on guilt and grief. It is a farewell in music.

Spiritual madrigal

Secular madrigal art turned to sacred penitence — the two halves of his life reconciled at the end.

Tears & remorse

Each movement dwells on Peter's weeping — restrained, searching, harmonically subtle music of contrition.

A composer's last word

Completed in 1594, the year he died — a valediction of extraordinary intensity and control.

11 — THE BAVARIAN COURT

Forty Years in Munich

For four decades Lassus led the chapel of the Bavarian dukes at Munich — first under Albrecht V, a lavish patron of the arts, then his successor. The court gave him one of the finest musical establishments in Europe and the freedom to write on the grandest scale.

A princely chapel

Skilled singers and instrumentalists at his command, and generous funds for lavish, illuminated manuscripts.

His fame drew visitors and commissions from across the continent to this Bavarian capital.

Honoured everywhere

The most celebrated musician of his generation, courted by rulers and honoured by pope and emperor alike.

Prints of his music circulated from Antwerp to Venice, spreading his style across Europe.

Stability at Munich let a restless, universal talent build an output of staggering size and range.

12 — TWO POLES OF AN AGE

Palestrina & Lassus

The two supreme masters of the later sixteenth century were exact contemporaries — and near-opposites. To hear one against the other is to hear the two directions Renaissance polyphony could take.

Both born in the 1530s, both dead in 1594 — the calm and the fire of the late Renaissance, side by side.

13 — LISTENING GUIDE

How to Listen to Lassus

  • Follow the words — Lassus always sets the meaning; watch the music mimic each image and mood.
  • Listen for the chromatic shivers at moments of grief, fear or wonder — notes sliding by half-steps.
  • Enjoy the contrast: from one line to the next he can swing between solemnity and laughter.
  • Sample his range — a Penitential Psalm, then a comic chanson — to feel the breadth of one imagination.

14 — LEGACY

The Divine Orlando

Lassus stands at the summit and the sunset of the Franco-Flemish tradition that had ruled European music for a hundred years. No one matched his combination of volume, variety and dramatic power. His pictorial word-setting and daring chromaticism pointed toward the expressive madrigal and, beyond it, the coming Baroque — even as his mastery of counterpoint crowned everything the Renaissance had learned.

Universality

He proved one composer could master every genre and language of Europe — the model of the cosmopolitan musician.

Expression

His pictorial, text-driven drama and chromatic daring opened the road to the madrigal and the Baroque.

Culmination

The last great Franco-Flemish master — the tradition's brilliant close, and its widest embrace.

"Carmina chromatico quae audis modulata tenore — the songs which you hear, modulated in a chromatic strain, these are they in which our twelve Sibyls once sang the secrets of salvation."

— the Latin prologue to Lassus's Prophetiae Sibyllarum
