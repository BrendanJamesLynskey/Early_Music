Byzantine Chant

c. 6th century – present · The Sacred Song of the Christian East

The sung theology of the Greek-speaking Eastern Roman Empire — and, to this day, of the Greek Orthodox Church and Mount Athos. A single unaccompanied voice, floating over a drone, colouring the octave with intervals no piano can play.

History of Early Music · The Christian East — No. 01

01 — DEFINITION

What Is Byzantine Chant?

Byzantine chant is the sacred music of the Greek-speaking Eastern Roman (“Byzantine”) Empire, and the living chant of the Orthodox Church of the East. It grew up parallel to — and largely independent of — the Latin plainchant of the West, sharing scriptural roots but developing its own sound-world: purely vocal, modal, and above all microtonal.

02 — THE MODES

The Oktōēchos — Eight Modes, Eight Weeks

Every chant belongs to one of eight ēchoi (modes). Four are authentic (kýrioi) and four are their plagal partners (plágioi) — a lower-lying reflection of each. The eight rotate week by week through the church year in a cycle whose organisation is traditionally credited to St John of Damascus (c. 675–749).

The Western eight modes and the Byzantine Oktōēchos are cousins — both eight, both authentic-and-plagal — but the eastern ēchoi carry stock melodies and microtonal genera the Latin modes never had.

03 — THE SOUND ITSELF

Microtonality & the Genera

This is the single most important thing to hear. Byzantine modes divide the octave far more finely than the West — modern theory counts 72 moria (commas) to the octave. Melodies move through four genera, each cutting the tetrachord differently.

Diatonic

The most familiar — near the Western whole-and-half-tone steps, the backbone of the festal modes.

Soft chromatic

The exotic colour: a narrow step, then a very wide middle step, then narrow again. Ēchos II.

Hard chromatic

An even more pungent augmented-second leap — the sound of lament and the Passion.

Enharmonic

Uses the finest divisions of all, quarter-tone-scale shadings around the diatonic degrees.

04 — THE DRONE

The Ison — Singing Over a Held Breath

Beneath the melody, a second group — the isokrátes — holds a single unchanging note: the ison. It is not harmony and not a chord; it is a sustained ground that anchors the ear to the mode’s foundation while the chanter’s line rises and falls above it.

The effect is of a voice suspended in a vast, resonant space — the melody made luminous by the drone it never leaves. The ison shifts only when the mode’s centre of gravity shifts, marking the great structural pillars of a chant.

05 — THE FORMS

The Great Hymn-Forms

Byzantine hymnography built a vast library of poetic-musical forms, each with its place in the liturgy — from terse refrains to enormous sung sermons, and finally to wordless ecstasy.

Troparion & Sticheron

Short stanzas inserted among psalm verses — the everyday building-blocks of the daily office.

Kontákion

The great early form: a sung metrical sermon of many stanzas, perfected by St Romanos the Melodist.

Kanon

Nine linked odes modelled on the biblical canticles; each ode opens with a model stanza, the heirmos.

Cherubikon

The Cherubic Hymn of the Divine Liturgy — slow, exalted, sung as the gifts are carried in procession.

Akathist

A celebrated hymn to the Mother of God, sung standing — twenty-four stanzas, each on a letter of the alphabet.

Teretismata

Wordless jubilant melismas sung to syllables like te-ri-rem — an ecstatic, textless praise beyond words.

06 — THE HYMNOGRAPHERS · I

St Romanos the Melodist

St Romanos the Melodist (c. 490–556), a deacon of Syrian origin who served in Constantinople under the Emperor Justinian, is the greatest of all Byzantine hymnographers and the supreme master of the kontákion.

His kontakia are sung metrical sermons — dramatic, tender, theologically dense. Best known is his Nativity kontakion, ἡ Παρθένος σήμερον (“Today the Virgin…”). Tradition says the gift of song came to him in a vision on the feast of the Nativity.

♪ Listen: a Romanos kontakion (recording)

The kontakion’s scale later shrank in the liturgy to a single surviving stanza and refrain — but in Romanos’s age it was the towering poetic form of the Christian East.

07 — THE HYMNOGRAPHERS · II

St Kassia — The Earliest Woman Composer

St Kassia (Kassiani, c. 810 – before 865) was a Constantinopolitan noblewoman who became a nun, abbess, poet and composer. She is one of the earliest known composers whose music survives under their own name — and the earliest known woman composer in the Western manuscript tradition.

The Hymn of Kassiani

Her most famous work, sung at Matins of Holy Wednesday, gives voice to the sinful woman who anointed Christ’s feet — a masterpiece of penitential feeling still central to Holy Week across the Orthodox world.

Poet as well as composer

Roughly fifty hymns and a body of epigrams are attributed to her — words and music by the same hand, at a time when named authorship of either was rare.

“Lord, the woman who had fallen into many sins, perceiving Thy divinity… taketh upon herself the myrrh in lamentation.”

08 — THE FLOWERING

Koukouzelis & the Kalophonic Style

St John Koukouzelis (c. 1280 – 1360s), “the Master” (maistor), was a monk of Mount Athos and the central figure of the florid kalophonic (“beautiful-sounding”) style — chant elaborated into long, virtuosic melismas, including whole passages of wordless teretismata.

The maïstor

A singer of legendary skill whose name became a byword for the art of the psáltis (cantor) itself.

Kalophonic chant

The plain liturgical melodies expanded into ecstatic, ornate settings — art-music for the greatest feasts.

Notational reform

Central to refining the middle-Byzantine neumes and to the “wheel” diagram of the modes that bears his name.

09 — NOTATION · I

Reading Byzantine Neumes — Motion, Not Position

Here is the payoff — and the deepest contrast with the West. Byzantine neumes are intervallic and gestural, not pitch-on-a-staff. Each sign tells you how far and which way to move from the note you are on — never an absolute pitch. You read left to right, and the relative motions accumulate.

How to read it: the martyría at the left names the mode (Ēchos I) and your starting degree (Pa). After that, no sign shows a fixed pitch — the íson says “stay”, olígon “up a step”, apóstrophos “down a step”, petastí “up, with a lift”. The grey dots are only the result. Contrast the Western staff, where a note’s height on the lines fixes its pitch outright.

10 — NOTATION · II

The 1814 Reform & the Pa–Vou–Ga Ladder

In 1814, the “New Method” of the three teachers — Chrysanthos of Madytos, Gregorios the Protopsaltis and Chourmouzios the Archivist — systematised the old neumes into the analytical notation still taught today.

They also fixed the Byzantine solfège: the seven ascending syllables Pa · Vou · Ga · Di · Ke · Zo · Ni. But note the crucial difference from the West: these name degrees of the mode, not absolute pitches. The staff pins a note to a line; Pa–Vou–Ga names where you are within the ēchos, and the true tuning of each step depends on the genus you are singing in.

11 — A LIVING TRADITION

Unbroken Song — Mount Athos & Beyond

Unlike Western plainchant, which had to be restored in the 19th century after centuries of decay, Byzantine chant has never fallen silent. It is sung today exactly as living practice, above all on the monastic republic of Mount Athos and in every Greek Orthodox parish.

The psáltis

The cantor stands at his stand (analogion) and leads; the ison is held by others around him. An oral art passed master to pupil.

Mount Athos

Twenty monasteries where the full round of chant is sung daily, and the tradition of the maïstores is guarded.

A wider family

The same system underlies Arabic, Romanian, Bulgarian and Serbian Orthodox chant — Byzantine song adapted to many tongues.

Where Latin chant is a recovered repertory, Byzantine chant is a continuous one — a thread of song running unbroken from Justinian’s Constantinople to a monastery choir tonight.

12 — EAST & WEST

Shared Roots, Divergent Paths

East and West began from the same soil — Jewish psalmody, the early Church, monophonic sacred song, eight modes, neumes that show gesture before pitch. Then they parted. Compare the same rising-and-falling shape in the two worlds: the Western tempered version, then the Byzantine soft chromatic.

The West went vertical

Toward polyphony and eventually equal temperament — many independent voices, harmony, the staff pinning every pitch, the tempered twelve-note scale.

The East went horizontal

Toward an ever more refined single line over the ison — microtonal genera, oral mastery, the voice alone, unbroken to the present day.

13 — LISTENING GUIDE

How to Listen to Byzantine Chant

  • Find the ison first — the unmoving drone underneath. Once you hear it, the melody’s every rise and fall snaps into focus.
  • Listen for the “impossible” intervals — steps that sound bent or exotic to a Western ear. That is the microtonal genus, not a singer out of tune.
  • Notice there are no instruments and no chords — only voices, one line and a drone. The richness is all in melody and colour.
  • Let the teretismata wash over you: when the words dissolve into te-ri-rem, the chant has passed beyond language into pure praise.

14 — LEGACY

The Sung Theology of the East

Byzantine chant is the great parallel to the Western tradition — a thousand-year art that chose the voice alone, the single line over the ison, and a microtonal palette the West set aside. Its neumes ask you not where a note sits but how the voice should move; its modes carry not just scales but whole worlds of feeling. It is theology you can hear.

Voice & ison

An aesthetic of pure vocal sound over a drone that has shaped Orthodox worship for fifteen centuries.

Microtonality

A living memory that music can divide the octave far more finely than twelve tempered notes.

Named composers

From Romanos to Kassia to Koukouzelis — among the earliest composers, and the earliest woman composer, known by name.
