Thomas Tallis

c. 1505–1585 · The English Survivor of the Reformation

The composer who served four Tudor monarchs and bent his art through the whiplash of the English Reformation — from Latin splendour to plain English prayer and back — and who conjured a motet in forty independent voices.

History of Early Music · No. 12 — The Tudor Church

01 — THE MAN

A Life Built for Survival

Thomas Tallis (c. 1505–1585) spent his long career at the heart of English music while the ground shifted violently beneath it. As the crown lurched between Catholic and Protestant, the very language, style and purpose of church music were remade — twice over. Tallis adapted to each turn without ever losing his voice, and died respected, wealthy, and still in royal service.

03 — THE WHIPLASH

Four Monarchs, Four Styles

No English composer had to reinvent his craft so often. Each reign redefined what church music was allowed to be — and Tallis wrote convincingly in every one of these opposing styles.

Henry VIII

Break with Rome, but the liturgy stays Latin. Rich, florid Catholic polyphony in the old English style.

Edward VI

Radical Protestant reform. English texts, homophony, one note per syllable — the birth of the anthem.

Mary I

Catholicism restored. Latin and elaborate polyphony come flooding back for five brief years.

Elizabeth I

The middle way. Latin motets tolerated at court alongside English service music; stability at last.

To be caught in the wrong style at the wrong moment could cost a musician his post — or worse. Tallis read the weather perfectly.

04 — HIS POSITION

Servant of the Chapel Royal

From the 1540s Tallis was a Gentleman of the Chapel Royal — the elite body of singers and organists that travelled with the sovereign and provided music for the crown's own worship. He served as organist and composer, at the very centre of English musical life.

It was the ideal vantage point for a survivor: close enough to the throne to sense each change of policy before it broke, and valued enough to be kept on through every upheaval. There he also taught the young William Byrd, his greatest pupil and future partner.

The Chapel Royal

Not a building but an institution — a corps of the finest singers and keyboard players in England.

Its Gentlemen sang the daily services of the monarch's household and provided music for coronations, funerals and state occasions.

Tallis and Byrd are jointly commemorated as its two greatest Tudor composers.

Byrd's own later greatness — Latin Masses for recusant Catholics alongside Anglican service music — grew directly out of Tallis's example.

05 — THE REFORMED IDEAL

"A Plain and Distinct Note"

When Edward VI's reformers demanded English-language worship, they wanted the words to be understood. Archbishop Thomas Cranmer set out the new ideal for church music: not the old clouds of overlapping Latin, but clear, syllabic English that any congregation could follow.

"The song that shall be made thereunto would not be full of notes, but, as near as may be, for every syllable a note; so that it may be sung distinctly and devoutly."

— Thomas Cranmer, Archbishop of Canterbury, letter to Henry VIII, 1544

Tallis answered this challenge better than anyone — and the English anthem was born from it. His response, If ye love me, is the model of the reformed style.

06 — THE ENGLISH ANTHEM

If ye love me — Words You Can Hear

Tallis's four-part anthem If ye love me (published 1565) is the reformed ideal made beautiful. It opens in homophony — all voices moving together in block chords, one syllable at a time — so every word rings out clearly, before a gentle flowering of imitation at the close. Sweetness without excess.

07 — PATRONAGE & PRINT

1575 — A Royal Monopoly

In 1575 Elizabeth I granted Tallis and William Byrd an extraordinary privilege: a royal monopoly on printing polyphonic music and the ruled paper to write it on, for twenty-one years. It was a mark of the crown's esteem — and a bold commercial venture.

The patent

The exclusive right to print and sell part-music in England, granted jointly to master and pupil.

Commercially it struggled at first — the English market for printed polyphony was still small.

Cantiones Sacrae (1575)

Their first publication under the patent: a joint volume of Latin motets, each composer contributing works to mark the seventeen years of Elizabeth's reign.

A dignified display of Latin sacred music, dedicated to the Queen.

That Latin motets could be dedicated to a Protestant queen shows the pragmatic breadth of the Elizabethan settlement — and of Tallis himself.

08 — THE GREAT MOTET

Spem in alium — Forty Voices in One

Tallis's most astonishing achievement is the motet Spem in alium — "Hope in any other" — scored for forty independent voices, arranged as eight choirs of five singers (soprano, alto, tenor, baritone, bass). No mere doubling: every one of the forty parts is its own line.

8 × 5 = 40

Eight five-part choirs, each a complete vocal group, spread in a great arc around the listener.

Antiphony

Sound is tossed from choir to choir — voices enter in a wave, roll around the room, then fall silent as the next choir answers.

The great tutti

At climactic moments all forty voices sound at once — an overwhelming wall of harmony unique in the period.

It may have been written c. 1570, possibly for a royal birthday; the exact occasion is debated. However it came about, nothing else in Renaissance England attempts anything on this scale.

09 — READING THE PAGE · I

Reading the Eight-Choir Grid

Picture the forty voices as a grid: eight choirs (I–VIII), five voices each. The music does not move all at once — a phrase begins in Choir I and passes hand to hand around the ensemble, like a wave, before the tutti.

How to read it: each box is one five-voice choir; each dot is a real, separate singer's line. Follow the highlighted choirs across the arc — the ear hears the sound travel through space, then collapse into the great forty-part tutti. It is architecture you can hear.

10 — READING THE PAGE · II

What Is a Canon?

A canon (or round) is the strictest kind of imitation: one melody, sung by two or more voices, each starting at a different time. The second voice sings exactly the same notes as the first, just entering later — so the tune harmonises with itself.

How to read it: the blue and purple noteheads trace the identical up-and-down shape — the purple line is simply the blue one copied and slid to the right. Where they overlap (after the green dashed line), the single tune is sounding against itself in harmony. That overlap is the whole magic of a canon.

11 — SING IT YOURSELF

Tallis's Canon

Tallis wrote one of the best-loved tunes in the English-speaking world: the hymn melody known as Tallis's Canon, sung to the evening words "Glory to thee, my God, this night." It is a genuine strict round — a second voice can enter partway through and sing the very same tune, and the two lock together in harmony. It is still sung today, four and a half centuries on.

First the melody by itself; then a second voice enters two bars later singing the same notes — the tune harmonising with itself

12 — LATIN SPLENDOUR

The Lamentations of Jeremiah

Alongside his plain English anthems, Tallis never abandoned the grave beauty of Latin polyphony. His two sets of Lamentations of Jeremiah are among the supreme achievements of Tudor sacred music — dark, richly interwoven settings of the prophet's grief over fallen Jerusalem, each verse marked by the Hebrew letters (Aleph, Beth…) set as their own melismatic flourishes.

Restrained grief

Slow-moving, close-woven counterpoint that broods rather than blazes — sorrow made sound.

The Hebrew letters

Each section of text opens with a Hebrew alphabet letter, given its own expressive melismatic setting.

For private devotion

Likely intended for domestic or contemplative use under Elizabeth, not the public liturgy.

13 — A LONG ECHO

The Third Mode Melody

In 1567 Tallis contributed nine psalm tunes to Archbishop Parker's English psalter. The third of them, in the Phrygian mode (Mode III), is a haunting melody that would echo down the centuries: in 1910 Ralph Vaughan Williams built his Fantasia on a Theme by Thomas Tallis upon it — a work that carried Tallis's name to concert halls worldwide.

14 — LISTENING GUIDE

How to Listen to Tallis

  • In the English anthems, listen for the clarity of the words — homophony first, then a little imitation blossoming at the cadence.
  • In Spem in alium, follow the sound travelling around the choirs, then brace for the full forty-voice tutti.
  • In the Latin works, savour the unhurried counterpoint — nothing is rushed; every suspension is allowed to ache and resolve.
  • Hear the canon in the hymn tune: once you notice the follower, you cannot un-hear it.

15 — LEGACY

The One Who Endured

Tallis is the living thread that runs unbroken through the whole English Reformation — proof that an artist could survive upheaval without hollowing out. He handed his craft to William Byrd, and through Byrd to the whole golden age of English music. His anthems still open cathedral services; his canon is still sung at bedtime; and Spem in alium still stops audiences' breath.

The English anthem

His reformed, word-clear style founded a tradition of Anglican church music that continues unbroken today.

A pupil's legacy

Through William Byrd, Tallis's art shaped the entire Elizabethan and Jacobean flowering of English music.

A monument in sound

Spem in alium remains the towering feat of Renaissance polyphony — forty voices, one vision.

"As he did live, so also did he die, in mild and quiet sort — O happy man!"

— from the epitaph on Tallis's memorial brass, formerly in St Alfege, Greenwich
